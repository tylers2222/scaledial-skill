# Storage and delivery crossovers

Scope: storing user-uploaded files, delivering them to viewers in many regions, making resized image copies, searching text in user content, and keeping cold objects cheap.
Use: open this file only after the components of the input design are listed. Use it to look up bands, costs, and sources. Do not use it to decide which components to discuss.

## Store user-uploaded files
Contract that matters: an upload the app acknowledged is never lost; the uploader can read it back at once; any app server can read any file; objects range from a few KB to many GB; cost grows with bytes stored, not with servers owned.
Axes that usually bind: durability, data_volume, team_ops_capacity

| Band | Option family (examples) | Why it fits | Breaks when | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| From the first upload to tens of petabytes, with any number of app servers | Managed object store (S3, Google Cloud Storage, Azure Blob Storage, Cloudflare R2) | Keep across the whole range. One provider documents 99.999999999% designed durability across at least 3 zones; strong read-after-write for new objects, overwrites, and deletes; at least 3,500 writes and 5,500 reads per second per key prefix, with no limit on prefixes; and $0.023 per GB-month for the first 50 TB (us-east-1 list price, September 2026). Nothing to provision or patch. Dropbox kept its file content on such a store while it grew to about 40 PB (2012) | Not inside this range. In the published cases, custom blob stores came at hundreds of billions of files or hundreds of petabytes, built by dedicated storage teams (see Traps) | source: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/storage-class-intro.html ; source: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/Welcome.html#ConsistencyModel ; source: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/optimizing-performance.html ; source: https://pricing.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/offers/v1.0/aws/AmazonS3/current/us-east-1/index.json ; source: https://dropbox.tech/infrastructure/magic-pocket-infrastructure |

Traps:
- Files on the app server's local disk look simpler, but they break at the second app server (each file lives on one machine) and at the first lost disk. Here the object store is the simple option, not the big one.
- Image bytes in the relational database: small blobs read fast (about 10 KB blobs read about 35% faster from one embedded database than from separate files; a 2006 study put objects under 256 KB in the database and over 1 MB in the filesystem), but every photo then rides through database backups, replicas, and failovers (source: https://www.sqlite.org/fasterthanfs.html ; source: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/to-blob-or-not-to-blob-large-object-storage-in-a-database-or-a-filesystem/).
- A custom blob store answers a hyperscale problem. Facebook built one at over 260 billion images (20 PB) because its NAS design needed about 3 disk operations per photo read, 2 of them for metadata. Dropbox moved over 90% of 500 PB in-house in a project of about two and a half years (source: https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/osdi10/tech/full_papers/Beaver.pdf ; source: https://dropbox.tech/infrastructure/magic-pocket-infrastructure).

## Deliver files to viewers in many regions
Contract that matters: viewers get the current version of a file; private files stay private (signed or authenticated access); deletes and privacy changes take effect within a stated time; pages stay inside their latency budget; one popular file cannot overload the origin.
Axes that usually bind: fan_out, geography, cost

| Band | Option family (examples) | Why it fits | Breaks when | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Viewers in one region near the store, and few reads per file | Serve from the object store with pre-signed URLs, or through the app | One system and one access-control model; the store is strongly consistent, so deletes and privacy changes apply at once. The first 100 GB a month of internet egress is free, then $0.09/GB for the first 10 TB (us-east-1); small-object first-byte latency is roughly 100–200 ms | Fan-out or distance grows. Every re-read pays egress plus a full round trip to the storage region (fiber minimum 56 ms New York–London and 160 ms New York–Sydney; 200–300 ms New York–Sydney in practice), and one viral file can reach its prefix's read rate (5,500/s, with 503 errors while the store scales) | source: https://aws.amazon.com/s3/pricing/ ; source: https://pricing.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/offers/v1.0/aws/AWSDataTransfer/current/us-east-1/index.json ; source: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/optimizing-performance.html ; source: https://hpbn.co/primer-on-latency-and-bandwidth/ |
| Many reads per file, or viewers on several continents | Split: object store as origin, CDN for reads (CloudFront, Cloudflare, Fastly, Akamai), content-hashed URLs, long browser cache lifetimes | A popular file leaves the origin once per cache lifetime, not once per viewer, and hits are served near the viewer. One CDN does not charge for fetches from same-provider origins; egress is free for the first 1 TB a month, then $0.085/GB (US and Europe); flat-rate plans start at $0 a month. Instagram (2011: 14 million users, 3 engineers, several TB of photos) used this pair to speed up image loads in Japan, its second-largest country | The long tail: old or rarely viewed files miss at each edge, so the origin sees those reads again. Facebook measured a 58% edge hit ratio, and its storage tier served almost 80% of requests for the least popular photos | source: https://aws.amazon.com/cloudfront/pricing/pay-as-you-go/ ; source: https://aws.amazon.com/cloudfront/pricing/ ; source: https://medium.com/instagram-engineering/what-powers-instagram-hundreds-of-instances-dozens-of-technologies-adf2e22da2ad ; source: https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~qhuang/papers/sosp_fbanalysis.pdf |
| Very high fan-out with a long tail across many edge locations | Add a mid-tier cache between edges and origin (origin shield, tiered or regional cache, own origin cache) | Misses from many edges collapse into one origin fetch. Facebook's origin cache hit 31.8% of the requests that reached it, and browser, edge, and origin caches together served about 90% of photo requests. One CDN charges $0.0075 per 10,000 shield requests (US) | Last band. One provider says a shield may not fit content with low cacheability or content that is requested infrequently | source: https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~qhuang/papers/sosp_fbanalysis.pdf ; source: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonCloudFront/latest/DeveloperGuide/origin-shield.html ; source: https://aws.amazon.com/cloudfront/pricing/pay-as-you-go/ |

Traps:
- Fan-out per file decides, not total traffic. Files read once (private uploads, backups) give an edge cache little to hit, but still bring signed URLs or cookies, cache rules, and stale copies to manage.
- The split adds a consistency gap. The store is strongly consistent; an edge copy can outlive a delete or a privacy change until its cache lifetime ends or an invalidation finishes. Give new versions new keys; one CDN charges $0.005 per invalidation path after 1,000 free paths a month (source: https://aws.amazon.com/cloudfront/pricing/pay-as-you-go/).
- Browser caching is free capacity: in Facebook's month-long trace of desktop browsers, the browser cache served 65.5% of photo requests (source: https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~qhuang/papers/sosp_fbanalysis.pdf).
- At small volume, price does not justify the split: the first tiers differ by about 5% ($0.09 versus $0.085 per GB). Latency and origin load do. The gap widens at hundreds of TB a month (source: https://aws.amazon.com/cloudfront/pricing/pay-as-you-go/ ; source: https://pricing.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/offers/v1.0/aws/AWSDataTransfer/current/us-east-1/index.json).

## Make resized copies of images
Contract that matters: every size a layout requests exists, or is made, before a viewer waits on it; orientation and color survive; copies follow the original's access rules; deleting the original deletes its copies.
Axes that usually bind: variant_count, change_rate, cost

| Band | Option family (examples) | Why it fits | Breaks when | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A few fixed sizes (Facebook stored 4), at any upload rate | Eager resizing: a background worker writes each size once after upload, next to the original (libvips or sharp, ImageMagick, Pillow) | One decode per upload, and every view is a plain cached file read. Work per photo is independent, so throughput grows by adding workers: one benchmark measured about 88 JPEG resizes per second on one 4-vCPU cloud instance. Facebook ran this pattern at 120 million uploads a day | The size or format count grows or changes often (responsive layouts, new devices, new formats). Every new size needs a backfill over all stored originals, and storage grows as originals times sizes, mostly for copies nobody views | source: https://sharp.pixelplumbing.com/performance/ (conditions: 4-vCPU AMD instance; 2725x2225 JPEG resized to 720x588 with Lanczos 3, quality 80; sharp 0.35.0 with libvips 8.18.2; larger phone photos cost more per operation) ; source: https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/osdi10/tech/full_papers/Beaver.pdf |
| Many sizes or formats, or sizes that change often | On-demand transform on the read path behind the CDN, plus a few eager sizes for the hottest views (image CDN services, edge functions, own resizer tier) | Work follows the variants that viewers request, and a new size needs no backfill. Facebook resized at its origin-cache tier and cached each variant as its own object; in its traces, unbounded edge caches able to resize would reach hit ratios up to 89% (Los Angeles) and 94% (Washington, D.C.). One service bills $0.50 per 1,000 unique transformations a month after 5,000 free, and counts repeats in the same month once | Low reuse: when each variant is viewed about once, every view pays a transform and first-view latency, and per-transform bills grow as originals times sizes viewed | source: https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~qhuang/papers/sosp_fbanalysis.pdf ; source: https://developers.cloudflare.com/images/pricing/ |

Traps:
- Moving to an on-demand image service "because uploads grew". Upload volume is not the binding axis, because workers scale out. The count of sizes and formats is.
- Resizing on the storage machines couples two scaling axes. Facebook moved photo transformations off its storage hosts into a separate tier so that each could scale on its own (source: https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/osdi14/osdi14-paper-muralidhar.pdf).

## Search text in user content
Contract that matters: results never include items the searcher may not see; new or edited text becomes searchable within a stated delay; word forms match (stemming); results come back in the order the product needs (newest first, or by relevance); latency suits interactive use.
Axes that usually bind: team_ops_capacity, data_volume, consistency

| Band | Option family (examples) | Why it fits | Breaks when | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The searchable table lives on one database primary, and queries are selective or ordered by an indexed column such as time | Full-text index inside the relational database (PostgreSQL tsvector with GIN, MySQL FULLTEXT, SQLite FTS5) | No second store and no sync pipeline: text and index commit in one transaction, so a privacy change applies to the next search, and visibility is a join. In one published test on 2.3 million rows, a rare-term query took 3–100 ms, and ordering about 1 million matches by an indexed column took about 25 ms; on 34,000 rows every query took under 300 ms | Relevance ranking over common words: ranking 1,038,914 matches took 8–25 s in the same test, because ranking reads every match. Or the product needs typo tolerance, facets, or corpus-aware relevance (built-in ranking uses no global statistics). Or the table is sharded across database servers: Instagram moved geo-search off its database once its media rows were sharded | source: https://xata.io/blog/postgres-full-text-search-postgres-vs-elasticsearch (conditions: GIN-indexed column versus one search engine; five runs per query, best and worst reported; hardware not stated; the author calls the method rather unscientific) ; source: https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/textsearch-controls.html ; source: https://medium.com/instagram-engineering/what-powers-instagram-hundreds-of-instances-dozens-of-technologies-adf2e22da2ad |
| Sharded source data; relevance, typo tolerance, or facets required; or an index larger than one server | Dedicated search cluster fed by change data capture (Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Solr, Vespa) | Relevance with corpus statistics, fuzzy matching, and facets are built in; the index spreads over shards (vendor guidance: 10–50 GB and fewer than 200 million documents per shard); search load leaves the main database | Last band | source: https://www.elastic.co/docs/deploy-manage/production-guidance/optimize-performance/size-shards |

Traps:
- A cluster for a small table is the usual substitute-down case. The smallest layout that survives a node loss is two data nodes plus a tiebreaker node (source: https://www.elastic.co/docs/deploy-manage/production-guidance/availability-and-resilience/resilience-in-small-clusters); one managed service recommends three dedicated master nodes across three zones (source: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/opensearch-service/latest/developerguide/bp.html). Add a sync pipeline and a copy of the visibility rules in the index.
- Table size is the wrong axis; rows matched per ranked query is the right one. The same 2.3-million-row table answered a 2-match query in 3–100 ms and a 1,038,914-match query in 8–25 s. Ranking a 10,000-row sample instead cut it to 144–400 ms, with some loss of relevance (source: https://xata.io/blog/postgres-full-text-search-postgres-vs-elasticsearch).
- A separate index is eventually consistent: one engine refreshes every second by default, and the pipeline adds its own lag (source: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/opensearch-service/latest/developerguide/bp.html). Until it catches up, search can show a photo whose owner just made it private. Moving search to a read replica has the same problem through replica lag.

## Keep cold data at low cost
Contract that matters: every object comes back within the time the product promises for it (milliseconds for anything a user can open; hours only for copies no user opens directly); durability does not drop; deletes are honored.
Axes that usually bind: cost, access_frequency, object_size

| Band | Option family (examples) | Why it fits | Breaks when | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Up to a few TB; or mostly objects under 128 KB; or objects read about monthly or more | Keep everything in the default storage class | No retrieval fees, minimum durations, or transition charges. 1 TB costs about $23 a month in the default class and about $4 in an instant-archive class, so tiering it saves about $19 a month. Infrequent-access and instant-archive classes bill objects under 128 KB as 128 KB, so a 20 KB thumbnail costs about 3.5 times as much in the infrequent-access class as in the default class | Tens of TB of large objects whose reads fall with age | source: https://pricing.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/offers/v1.0/aws/AmazonS3/current/us-east-1/index.json ; source: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/storage-class-intro.html ; rule of thumb (band edge): tiering a few TB saves tens of dollars a month at these prices, which rarely repays the setup work and the retrieval-fee risk (conditions: us-east-1 list prices, objects of 128 KB or more, a small team) |
| Tens of TB or more of objects of 128 KB or larger that are read less as they age | Lifecycle rule to an infrequent-access or instant-archive class after N days, or an automatic-tiering class (S3 Standard-IA, Glacier Instant Retrieval, Intelligent-Tiering; GCS Nearline, Coldline; Azure Cool, Cold) | Photo reads decay with age: at Facebook, week-old content got about 10 times fewer requests than content under a day old for 8 of 9 types, one-year-old content over 100 times fewer, and photos counted as warm after 3 months. Per GB-month: $0.023 default, $0.0125 infrequent access, $0.004 instant archive, all with millisecond reads. Automatic tiering charges $0.0025 per 1,000 objects a month and no retrieval fee | Objects are read more often than the class assumes (about monthly for infrequent access, quarterly for instant archive; retrieval costs $0.01 and $0.03 per GB); objects are small (128 KB minimum, and automatic tiering skips them); or objects die young (30- and 90-day minimum charges) | source: https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/osdi14/osdi14-paper-muralidhar.pdf ; source: https://pricing.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/offers/v1.0/aws/AmazonS3/current/us-east-1/index.json ; source: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/storage-class-intro.html |
| Copies no user opens directly: original uploads kept for export, legal holds, backups | Archive class with hours-long restore (S3 Glacier Deep Archive, GCS Archive, Azure Archive) | $0.00099 per GB-month; a standard restore finishes within 12 hours, a bulk restore within 48 | Any user-facing read touches it; or objects are small: each archived object adds 40 KB of billed metadata (8 KB of it at the default rate) and transitions cost $0.05 per 1,000, so archiving 1 million 20 KB thumbnails costs $50 to save about 20 cents a month | source: https://pricing.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/offers/v1.0/aws/AmazonS3GlacierDeepArchive/current/us-east-1/index.json ; source: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/restoring-objects-retrieval-options.html ; source: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/storage-class-intro.html |

Traps:
- Tiering thumbnails: small objects are billed as 128 KB in infrequent-access classes and carry 40 KB of metadata in archive classes. Keep thumbnails in the default class and tier only the originals (source: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/storage-class-intro.html).
- Age-based rules assume reads fall with age. Facebook kept profile photos out of its warm tier, because they stay hot for about a year (source: https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/osdi14/osdi14-paper-muralidhar.pdf).
- Early deletes still pay the minimum: 30 days (infrequent access), 90 (instant archive), 180 (deep archive) (source: https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/storage-class-intro.html). Facebook saw about 25% of photos deleted within a year, mostly young ones (source: https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/osdi10/tech/full_papers/Beaver.pdf).
- At hyperscale, the saving comes from storing fewer copies, not from a storage class: Facebook's warm tier cut its effective replication factor from 3.6 to 2.1 (source: https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/osdi14/osdi14-paper-muralidhar.pdf). That is a storage-team project, not a lifecycle rule.
